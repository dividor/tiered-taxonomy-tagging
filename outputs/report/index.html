Tiered taxonomy experiments · results to 14 November 2026

Which taxonomy taggers work best

We tested 23 ways of tagging documents with labels from a tiered taxonomy, on EU laws and on humanitarian excerpts. The most accurate was our combined method: several trained models each suggest labels, and a scorer trained on documents they never saw decides which to keep. Which models it draws on is chosen for each dataset. It led on the EU laws, and on HumSet 2.0.0 by the HumBERT paper's measure, though XR-Transformer edges it by exact labels, 0.722 to 0.716. Methods that prompt an LLM came well behind, though on the EU laws they found more of the rare labels.

Every method on the same documents

The results

Every method was run on the same 600 EU laws and on all 12,896 test excerpts of HumSet 2.0.0, the HumBERT paper's test set, and scored the same way. None of them saw these documents during training or tuning. Open a method's card, by its name, to read what it does and the papers behind it. Sort the table by any column heading.

What the numbers mean

F1 score
Balances finding the correct labels against adding wrong ones. 1.00 is perfect.
Exact labels
Credit only for the very label a person assigned. The main measure, and the hardest. On HumSet, a pillar given together with one of its own subpillars is dropped first, since the subpillar already names it; a pillar given alone stays.
Tier 1, 2, 3
Credit for landing in the right branch at that level of the taxonomy, even with the wrong label inside it. Tier 1 is the broadest, so the easiest.
Macro F1
HumSet only. The measure the HumBERT paper (Tamagnone et al., 2023) reports: F1 worked out for each label, so rare labels count as much as common ones, averaged in each of five groups of labels and then over the groups. Trained models get a threshold per label, chosen on development excerpts; LLM methods, which give labels without scores, count every label they return.
Rare labels
Recall, from 0 to 1: the share each method found of the correct labels in the least-used quarter of labels in training, and of those never given in training.
Cost
LLM methods: what the calls cost per 1,000 documents. Trained methods: one-off GPU time, or free on a laptop.

Kinds of method

LLM
Prompts a general-purpose LLM. Needs no labelled documents.
LLM + examples
Prompts an LLM and shows it labelled training documents.
LLM + trained model
An LLM chooses from a shortlist that a trained model helped make.
No training
Embedding search only. Needs no labelled documents and no LLM.
Trained
Learns from the dataset's labelled documents. Trains on a laptop, except XR-Transformer.
Fine-tuned
A pretrained neural model further trained on the dataset, on a rented GPU.
Combined
Our method. Several trained models each suggest labels, and a scorer trained on documents they never saw decides which to keep. Which models it draws on is chosen for each dataset.

EU laws (MultiEURLEX): 600 test laws, EuroVoc's top three tiers

The laws are split by date, so the test laws are newer than the training laws. Rows marked "with newer laws" were also trained on newer laws from the development split, which brings them closer in time to the test laws. By exact labels the XR-Transformer has the top score, 0.722, against the combined method's 0.716; by macro F1 the combined method leads, 0.663 against 0.506. We have not tested whether the exact-labels gap is larger than chance.

We first ran every method on 1,500 sampled test excerpts of HumSet 1.0.0, an earlier version with different splits. There the combined method had the top score by exact labels, 0.662 against XR-Transformer's 0.642, and on all 15,147 of that version's test excerpts it beat HumBERT's recipe, rerun by us, by macro F1, 0.574 to 0.560.

LLM methods used openai/gpt-oss-120b:novita and openai/gpt-oss-20b:deepinfra through Hugging Face Inference Providers, except LLM-Ensemble, which used the five open models listed in configs/experiment.yaml. GPU training ran on Hugging Face Jobs at November 2026 prices.

What the results show

Findings

  • The combined method is the most accurate. On the laws it scores 0.566 by exact labels, against 0.497 for the best single model, the fine-tuned Legal-BERT. On HumSet 2.0.0 it leads by the HumBERT paper's macro F1, 0.663 against 0.506 for XR-Transformer, the best single model, though XR-Transformer edges it by exact labels, 0.722 to 0.716. A version of the combined method also has the top score at every tier.
  • Trained models beat LLM methods, with exceptions on HumSet. On the laws every fine-tuned model scores above every LLM method; the best LLM method, KI-FSPrompt, scores 0.340. On HumSet 2.0.0 KI-FSPrompt scores 0.585: below every trained model that has no LLM inside it, but above the fine-tuned reranker (0.542), GLiNER2.5 (0.502) and fine-tuned small LLM (0.493). KI-FSPrompt also needs labelled documents, because it shows them to the LLM as examples.
  • LLM methods fall further behind on finer labels. On the laws, KI-FSPrompt trails the best single model by 0.07 at tier 1 (0.68 against 0.75), and by 0.15 at tier 3 (0.54 against 0.69).
  • LLM methods find labels the training data lacks. The LLM keyword pipeline finds 49% of the correct labels never seen in training; the combined method finds 1%. But the pipeline suggests 15 labels per law, where a law has about 6.5, so most of its suggestions are wrong.
  • Showing the LLM labelled examples is the best LLM recipe. KI-FSPrompt beats the plain LLM keyword pipeline by 0.13 (95% interval 0.12 to 0.15) on the laws and by 0.17 (95% interval 0.16 to 0.19) on the HumSet 1.0.0 sample, at about half the cost. LLM-Ensemble, with five LLMs, did worse than the plain pipeline and cost about eight times as much.
  • Search-then-choose needs a trained model in the shortlist. On the laws, search alone put only 41% of the correct labels in its top 100, so the LLM could not choose the rest. Adding the trained model's suggestions raised that to 75%, and F1 by 0.08 (95% interval 0.06 to 0.09).
  • Walking down the taxonomy one branch at a time fails. It scores 0.02 on the laws. Keeping three branches open helps on both datasets. Starting from search results instead of from the top helps on the laws but hurts on the excerpts.
  • Asking the LLM to double-check its labels made things worse. F1 fell by 0.02 (95% interval 0.01 to 0.02) on the laws and by 0.02 (95% interval 0.02 to 0.03) on the HumSet 1.0.0 sample, and the number of calls doubled. Keeping only labels with a supporting quote also lowered F1.

Each difference with an interval was tested on the same documents: the 600 laws, or the sample of HumSet 1.0.0 where every method was first run. Every one of those intervals excludes zero, so none of these differences is likely to be chance.

On HumSet 2.0.0, the version HumBERT's published figures come from, the combined method scores 0.663 on the mean against the published 0.648, and its 95% interval, 0.657 to 0.670, lies above it. Its interval lies wholly above the published figure in Subpillars 1D and Subpillars 2D. It beats HumBERT's recipe, rerun with the authors' own code and settings, in every group.

Whether that rerun reproduces the published figures depends on how subpillar scores are averaged, which the paper (Tamagnone et al., 2023) does not say. Averaging every label once, as every score here does, it comes within 0.015 of every published group. Averaging subpillars within each pillar first, as the authors' code does, it falls short in Subpillars 1D (by 0.041), so notebook 13 records it as not reproducing the paper. The published figures have two decimals; their mean is worked out from them.

The HumBERT paper (Tamagnone et al., 2023) prints no mean of the five groups. Its own "Avg" column, 0.73, is the tier-1 average: the mean of Sectors, Pillars 1D and Pillars 2D, leaving out both subpillar groups, which score lowest. On that average the combined method scores 0.739 on 2.0.0, and the recipe rerun 0.722.

The recipe rerun is one run with one seed, so it has no interval.

Long documents

Labelling long documents in pieces

Long documents were cut into 400-word pieces, an LLM method labelled each piece, and four rules combined the pieces' labels into labels for the whole document. Weighting each piece by how sure the model was gave the same result as keeping any label found in any piece. Which rule worked best depended on the dataset.

Read with care

Limits

  • Most methods are simplified from their papers, so these results say little about the originals. Each method's card says how ours differs.
  • The EU laws table uses 600 laws, a sample of the test set; HumSet 2.0.0's table uses its whole test set. Small differences between neighbouring rows may not hold on other documents.
  • Some findings, marked with the HumSet 1.0.0 sample, were measured only on that earlier version, where every method was first run.
  • Results depend on the LLMs named above. Other LLMs could change the order of the LLM methods.
  • The second attempt was made after seeing the first attempt's test scores. The pass marks it was judged against were not changed.
